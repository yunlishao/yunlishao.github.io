Living Lab for Smart Intersections

Live at Sanford Drive and Cedar Street, UGA campus

Our living lab is a working campus intersection that we can watch and measure live. A panoramic traffic camera on the signal pole and an edge computer in the signal cabinet stream to our lab, where every car, bus, bike and person is tracked as it moves. We see the intersection as it is right now, replay any stored stretch, and measure safety, traffic and signal performance from real traffic. The same live feed is where we test our research: trajectory prediction, driver warnings and signal timing. Sanford and Cedar is the first site, and the platform is built to add more.

How it works

  1. 1Panoramic cameraVideo of the whole intersection from the signal pole
  2. 2Signal cabinetA switch links the camera, the roadside unit and the edge computer
  3. 3Lab serverFinds and follows every road user
  4. 4DashboardLive view, history and analytics

Every car, bus, bike and person is picked out in the camera video, followed across the intersection, and placed on the campus map to about a meter.

The traffic light is read from the video too, since the signal controller is not connected yet.

It is stored as well, so a past stretch can be played back, counted or exported. Video and tracks stay on the lab's secured systems.

Current capabilities

Live view and replay

The video, every road user's track and the signal state are recorded as they happen. The same dashboard that shows the intersection live can switch to any stored stretch and play it back, with the map and the camera views in step.

Automatically captured safety-critical events

Conflicts between vehicles, pedestrians and cyclists are detected from the live tracks. Each event is saved with its video clip and surrogate safety measures such as time to collision (TTC), post-encroachment time (PET) and closest distance, building a record of where and when conflicts recur. Turning movements and pedestrian volumes

Turning movement counts, turning ratios and pedestrian volumes are produced from the tracks for every approach and crosswalk and stored, so any period can be summarized at the time step a study needs. An example weekday from the dashboard:

Signal performance analytics

The signal state read from the video is paired with every tracked vehicle, giving cycle-by-cycle performance measures for each approach: arrivals on green, platoon ratio, vehicles stopped, average delay and coordination diagrams. They show how well the current timing serves real demand, using only the camera, with no detectors or controller connection.

Ongoing research

Trajectory prediction

Building on the live tracks, a learned occupancy model forecasts where every car, bus, bike and person will be over the next one to three seconds, from what the cameras have seen up to that moment [1, 2]. Short-horizon forecasts like these are what proactive applications need: warning a driver before a conflict, and adjusting the signal in real time.

How to read it: red is where the model expects road users to be. Each white box is where it expects a vehicle to be, each white circle a person.

From prediction to driver warnings

The first application of the forecasts: when a vehicle's predicted path meets a pedestrian's, the driver is alerted. Detection and prediction will run on the edge computer in the signal cabinet, keeping the delay short enough for the driver to react, first through a phone app and then over V2X through the roadside unit.

Top: what the driver would see. Bottom: the same kind of moment at this intersection.

Testing signal timing plans on real traffic

Real traffic from just after a class change is copied into a simulator and run five minutes ahead under four timing plans: the same vehicles and people, only the signal differs. A timing change can be judged on real demand before it reaches the field, a step toward real-time signal control.

Transit signal priority

Campus buses are picked out from the tracks, along with how often and how long the red light holds them. The V2X roadside unit on the pole and the signal controller in the cabinet are a few meters apart. With a link between them, an approaching bus could ask for a few more seconds of green.

Work with us

We are open to working with partners anywhere, and especially with UGA faculty, students and campus units, since the living lab is on campus. If your work involves traffic, safety, transit, connected and automated vehicles, real-time control or campus planning, we would be glad to talk.

The live system runs on a secured network to protect privacy and data, and we are happy to show it in person on campus or over a call.
